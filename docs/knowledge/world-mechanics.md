## 1. Money Is Created by Bank Lending

When a bank makes a loan, it does not transfer existing money. It creates
new money by simultaneously creating an asset (the loan) and a liability
(the deposit in the borrower's account). The deposit did not exist before
the loan. This is not fractional reserve lending in the textbook sense —
the Bank of England confirmed this in 2014 ("Money Creation in the Modern
Economy").

Central banks influence lending through interest rates (the price of money)
and reserve requirements, but they do not directly control the money supply.
This is why rate cuts sometimes fail — if banks won't lend and borrowers
won't borrow, cheap money sits idle. Pushing on a string.

**Common misconception:** "The Fed prints money and injects it into the
economy." The Fed creates reserves (bank-to-bank money), not deposits
(money people spend). QE swaps Treasuries for reserves — it changes the
composition of bank assets, not the amount of money in the real economy
directly. The transmission depends on banks lending those reserves out.

**Source:** Richard Werner, "Princes of the Yen" (2003). Bank of England
Quarterly Bulletin Q1 2014.

## 2. Investment Drives Growth, Not Consumption

GDP = C + I + G + (X-M) is an accounting identity, not a causal model.
Consumption being 70% of US GDP tells you what GDP IS, not what drives it.

The causal chain: Investment in productive capacity → increased productivity
→ more goods/services per unit of labor → higher wages → more consumption
→ GDP grows. Consumption is the result, not the cause.

Countries that stimulate consumption without investment (consumer credit
expansion, home equity extraction) get temporary sugar rushes followed by
debt crises. Countries that invest productively build durable wealth even
if consumption is temporarily suppressed.

**Common misconception:** "Consumer spending drives the economy, so we
should stimulate demand." This confuses the accounting identity with
causation. Demand stimulus without productive capacity creates inflation,
not growth.

**Source:** Michael Pettis, "The Great Rebalancing" (2013).

## 3. Not All Investment Is Productive

A factory producing goods people buy generates cash flows that service the
debt used to build it. That's productive investment. An apartment complex
with no residents generates zero cash flow. The debt used to build it can
only be serviced by taking on MORE debt or by asset appreciation — that's
unproductive investment disguised as GDP growth.

China built the equivalent of the entire US housing stock in 15 years. Much
of it sits empty. The construction showed up as GDP growth, but the
underlying economics produce bad debt, not wealth. Japan in the 1980s made
the same mistake with "bridges to nowhere" — infrastructure spending that
generated construction GDP but no economic return.

**The test:** Does this investment generate cash flows sufficient to service
the debt used to fund it? If yes, productive. If it depends on asset
appreciation or continued credit expansion, unproductive.

**Source:** Michael Pettis, "Avoiding the Fall" (2013). Richard Koo,
"The Holy Grail of Macroeconomics" (2008) — on Japan's balance sheet recession.

## 4. Capital Allocation Is the Highest-Leverage Function

Every economic outcome traces back to an allocation decision. Who decided
to fund this factory, this research program, this infrastructure project?
Central banks set the price of money. Commercial banks decide who gets
loans. Sovereign wealth funds, pension funds, hedge funds, and VCs
determine which companies and projects receive capital.

Capital allocation is more powerful than innovation. A brilliant invention
that can't get funded dies. A mediocre idea that gets $10B in capital
reshapes markets. This is literally what a hedge fund does — sit at the
allocation layer and direct capital toward the highest-return opportunities.

## 5. Every Country Operates on Different Rules

Do not apply US economic logic universally. Major frameworks:

**United States:** Consumer credit-driven economy + dollar hegemony +
deep capital markets + financialization. The US can run persistent trade
deficits because the world needs dollars. The Fed's decisions ripple
through every economy on Earth because dollar-denominated debt is
everywhere.

**China (中国):** State-directed investment + 土地财政 (land finance) +
export mercantilism + dual circulation. Local governments fund themselves
by selling land-use rights to developers — this created both rapid
urbanization and the property debt crisis. The PBoC manages the RMB
exchange rate as a strategic tool, not a market price. SOEs serve political
objectives, not shareholder value. The Party's survival is the optimization
function — economic policy follows from this constraint.

Key Chinese mechanics: local vs central fiscal relationship (地方政府 fund
themselves through land sales because tax revenue flows to 中央), Hong
Kong's role as the capital gateway (money enters/exits mainland China
through HK), the Great Firewall as an economic tool (protects domestic
tech monopolies), Belt and Road as surplus capacity export + geopolitical
positioning.

**Japan:** Yield curve control + aging demographics + carry trade dynamics.
The BoJ has kept rates near zero for 30 years because Japan's debt-to-GDP
(>250%) would become unserviceable at normal rates. The yen carry trade
(borrow cheap yen, invest in higher-yielding assets globally) means BoJ
policy directly affects US Treasury yields, emerging market capital flows,
and risk appetite globally. When the yen strengthens, carry trades unwind
and global risk assets sell off — this is why Japanese monetary policy
moves US markets.

**Gulf States:** Resource extraction + sovereign wealth + petrodollar
recycling. Saudi Arabia prices oil in USD (1974 agreement), generating
dollar demand globally. Surplus oil revenue is recycled into US Treasuries
and Western assets via sovereign wealth funds (PIF, ADIA, KIA). These
countries are transitioning (Saudi Vision 2030) but remain structurally
dependent on hydrocarbon revenue for decades.

**European Union:** Shared currency without shared fiscal policy. Germany
runs structural surpluses; Southern Europe runs structural deficits. The
ECB cannot set policy that works for both simultaneously. This is a
permanent, unresolvable tension that produces periodic crises (2010-2012
eurozone crisis, recurring Italian debt concerns).

**Source:** Michael Pettis on China. Richard Werner on Japan. Adam Tooze,
"Crashed" (2018) on the interconnected global system.

## 6. Follow Flows, Not Narratives

Capital flows are observable facts: fund flows data, 13F filings, central
bank balance sheets, trade data, SWIFT transaction volumes. Narratives are
stories people construct around facts — they lag reality and serve the
storyteller's interests.

When flows and narrative diverge, trust the flows. If everyone says "buy
tech" but fund flow data shows institutional money rotating to energy,
the institutions know something the narrative hasn't caught up to.

Key flow sources: Fed H.4.1 (weekly balance sheet), TIC data (international
capital flows), ICI fund flow data, 13F filings (quarterly institutional
holdings), CFTC Commitment of Traders (futures positioning).

## 7. The Dollar System

Five reinforcing pillars:

**a) Petrodollar:** Saudi Arabia prices oil in USD since 1974 (the
petrodollar agreement). Every country that imports oil needs dollars.
This creates permanent, structural dollar demand independent of US
economic performance.

**b) SWIFT + Dollar Clearing:** ~40% of international payments go through
SWIFT in USD. The US has weaponized this (Iran sanctions, Russia 2022).
Being cut off from dollar clearing is economic death for any country.
This is soft power backed by infrastructure, not military force.

**c) US Military:** Secures global shipping lanes (12 carrier strike
groups, bases in 80+ countries). The implicit deal: the US provides
security for global trade; in return, trade is denominated in dollars.
Countries that try to leave the dollar system face security implications.

**d) Capital Market Depth:** US Treasury market is the deepest, most
liquid market on Earth (~$25T outstanding). When institutions need a
"risk-free" asset, there is no alternative with comparable liquidity.
This makes Treasuries the foundation of global collateral chains.

**e) Network Effect:** Everyone uses dollars because everyone uses dollars.
Switching costs are enormous. De-dollarization requires building
alternative payment infrastructure, trade agreements, and reserve assets
simultaneously. China's CIPS, Russia's SPFS, and BRICS settlement
discussions are real but collectively handle a fraction of dollar volume.

Dollar share of global reserves: ~71% (2000) → ~58% (2024). Declining
but slowly. There is no single replacement — only fragmentation among
EUR, CNY, gold, and bilateral arrangements.

**Source:** Michael Hudson, "Super Imperialism" (2003). Zoltan Pozsar,
"War and Interest Rates" (2022).

## 8. Energy Is the Master Resource

Every economic activity requires energy. Oil is not just fuel:
- Plastics (packaging, electronics, medical devices, construction)
- Fertilizers (ammonia → nitrogen fertilizer → global food supply)
- Pharmaceuticals (petroleum-derived compounds)
- Lubricants (every machine with moving parts)
- Asphalt (every road)
- Petrochemicals (solvents, detergents, synthetic rubber)

World consumption: ~100 million barrels/day. Even aggressive renewable
transitions only displace the FUEL portion of oil demand (~60%). The
petrochemical portion (~30%) has no viable substitute at scale.

China's renewable push is primarily a STRATEGIC move — energy independence
from US-controlled shipping lanes (Malacca Strait). China imports ~70%
of its oil, mostly through Malacca. A US naval blockade would cripple
China's economy within weeks. Domestic renewables + EVs reduce this
vulnerability. The environmental narrative is real but secondary to
the strategic calculus.

**Source:** Vaclav Smil, "Energy and Civilization" (2017). Daniel Yergin,
"The New Map" (2020).

## 9. Geography Is Destiny

Critical chokepoints:
- **Strait of Hormuz:** 20% of global oil, 25% of global LNG. Iran can
  threaten closure. US Navy's 5th Fleet is permanently stationed in Bahrain
  specifically for this.
- **Strait of Malacca:** 25% of global trade, 80% of China/Japan/Korea
  oil imports. Whoever controls Malacca controls East Asian energy security.
- **Taiwan Strait:** TSMC produces ~90% of the world's most advanced
  semiconductors. A Chinese invasion or blockade would collapse the global
  tech supply chain immediately.
- **Suez Canal:** Europe-Asia shipping shortcut. Closure (2021 Ever Given,
  2024 Houthi attacks) adds 10+ days to shipping routes, spiking costs.
- **GIUK Gap:** North Atlantic chokepoint between Greenland, Iceland, UK.
  Controls submarine access between Arctic and Atlantic. NATO's undersea
  surveillance network.
- **South China Sea:** $3.4T in trade transits annually. China's artificial
  islands are military installations controlling this flow.

## 10. Demographics

**China:** Peak population reached ~2022. One-child policy (1979-2015)
created a demographic time bomb: working-age population is shrinking,
elderly population is exploding. UN projects China could fall below 800M
by 2100. No policy can reverse this — the women who would need to have
children today were never born. This constrains GDP growth structurally
regardless of any other policy.

**Japan:** Demographic decline since ~2008. Population shrinking by
~500K/year. This drives: persistent deflation pressure, labor shortages,
fiscal strain from elderly care, and the BoJ's inability to normalize
rates (aging population = less consumption = less inflation).

**India:** Median age ~28 (vs China ~39, Japan ~49). Demographic dividend
ahead — large, young, increasingly educated workforce entering productive
years. If governance doesn't squander it, India is the structural
growth story of the next 30 years.

**United States:** Natural population growth slowing but immigration
offsets decline (if policy allows). The US is the only major developed
economy with a demographic structure that supports growth — contingent
on continued immigration.

**Source:** Peter Zeihan, "The End of the World Is Just the Beginning"
(2022) — controversial but useful for demographic-geographic framework.

## 11. Government Incentive Structures

All governments optimize for regime survival. The mechanism differs:

**Democracies:** Leaders optimize for re-election. This produces short-term
populism: stimulus before elections, deferred pain, policies that feel
good now and cost later. Fiscal discipline is structurally impossible
because voters punish austerity and reward spending.

**Single-party states (China):** The Party optimizes for stability and
continued rule. This produces: suppression of volatility (market circuit
breakers, property market interventions), long-term infrastructure
investment (no election cycles to interrupt), but also inability to allow
creative destruction (zombie SOEs kept alive for employment).

**Theocracies (Iran):** Supreme Leader optimizes for religious-political
legitimacy. Economic policy serves ideological goals. Sanctions resistance
becomes a point of pride, not just a constraint. Self-sufficiency
narratives serve domestic legitimacy even when economically inefficient.

**Petrostate monarchies (Saudi/UAE):** Royal family optimizes for
continued rule through social contract: no taxation, generous subsidies,
in exchange for no political representation. When oil revenue drops, the
social contract strains. Vision 2030-type reforms are preemptive regime
survival strategies.

Read every government action through this lens. "Irrational" behavior
becomes perfectly rational when you understand what they're optimizing for.

## 12. The Financial Promise Hierarchy

Money is not a thing — it is a hierarchy of promises with varying degrees
of credibility:

```
MOST CREDIBLE (closest to "real" money)
  │
  │  Cash (Federal Reserve notes)
  │  Central bank reserves
  │  Treasury bills (< 1 year)
  │  Treasury notes/bonds (1-30 years)
  │  Agency debt (Fannie/Freddie, implicit gov guarantee)
  │  Bank deposits (FDIC insured up to $250K)
  │  Money market funds
  │  Commercial paper (corporate short-term IOUs)
  │  Corporate bonds (investment grade)
  │  Corporate bonds (high yield / "junk")
  │  Equities (residual claim after all debt is paid)
  │  Derivatives (claims on claims)
  │  Crypto (promises backed by... consensus?)
  │
LEAST CREDIBLE
```

In a crisis, everyone tries to move UP this hierarchy simultaneously.
This is why: stocks crash → corporate bonds crash → money flows into
Treasuries → dollar strengthens. The dollar strengthens in crises EVEN
WHEN THE CRISIS ORIGINATES IN THE US because the world needs dollars
to settle obligations denominated in dollars.

**Source:** Perry Mehrling, "The New Lombard Street" (2010).

## 13. Credit Cycles

Short-term debt cycle (~5-10 years):
  Easy credit → asset price inflation → wealth effect → more spending →
  overheating → central bank tightens → credit contracts → asset prices
  fall → recession → central bank eases → repeat.

Long-term debt cycle (~75-100 years):
  Each short cycle ends with more total debt than the last because
  central banks lower rates further each time to stimulate recovery.
  Eventually rates hit zero and debt levels are so high that monetary
  policy loses effectiveness. This triggers deleveraging — either through
  austerity (painful), default (painful), money printing/inflation
  (hidden pain), or some combination. The last US long-cycle deleveraging
  was the Great Depression. The current cycle's debt levels suggest we're
  in the late stages.

**Source:** Ray Dalio, "How the Economic Machine Works" (2013, free video).
"Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order" (2021).

## 14. Supply Chain Fragility

Modern supply chains are optimized for efficiency, not resilience. Just-in-
time manufacturing minimizes inventory costs but maximizes vulnerability to
disruption. COVID proved this: a $0.50 chip shortage shut down $50,000 car
production lines.

Semiconductor supply chains are the deepest and most fragile:
- Design (US/UK): ARM, NVIDIA, AMD, Qualcomm, Apple
- EDA tools (US): Synopsys, Cadence, Mentor (3 companies, no alternatives)
- Equipment (Netherlands/Japan/US): ASML (monopoly on EUV lithography),
  Tokyo Electron, Applied Materials, Lam Research
- Fabrication (Taiwan/Korea): TSMC (~90% advanced logic), Samsung
- Packaging/substrate (Japan): Ajinomoto (ABF substrate, ~95% share),
  TOTO (ceramic processing), Ibiden, Shinko
- Memory (Korea/US): SK Hynix, Samsung, Micron
- Assembly/test (SE Asia): ASE, Amkor

A disruption at ANY point — earthquake in Taiwan, export controls on
ASML, Japan-Korea trade dispute — cascades through the entire chain.
Map the dependencies. Find the chokepoints. That's where the alpha is.
